# The Radcliffe Line: An Analysis of Errors, and a Reconstruction of the Optimal Boundary

*Analysis of Sir Cyril Radcliffe's 1947 Boundary Award (Punjab and Bengal), the specific
failures it contained, and what a better line would have looked like under the same mandate.*

---

## 0. Scope and method

Two questions are answered here:

1. **What did Radcliffe actually get wrong** — separating what was his fault from what was
   imposed on him.
2. **What was the optimal line** — defined against explicit criteria, drawn segment by
   segment, and costed honestly.

All population figures are from the **1941 Census of India**, which is the data Radcliffe
himself used. That census is itself a source of error (§2.1) and every number below should be
read with that caveat. Where Indian, Pakistani, and Bangladeshi historiography diverge on
motive, this is flagged rather than resolved — the documentary record supports the *facts* of
the line far better than it supports anyone's account of the *intent* behind it.

---

## 1. What was imposed on him (not his fault)

Any fair audit has to start here, because several of the most-repeated criticisms are
misdirected. Radcliffe did not choose:

| Constraint | Detail |
|---|---|
| **The deadline** | Mountbatten moved independence from June 1948 to August 1947. Radcliffe arrived in India **8 July 1947** and had roughly **five weeks** to partition a territory of ~88 million people. |
| **His own ignorance** | He was selected *because* he had never been to India — inexperience was treated as a guarantee of impartiality. |
| **The deadlocked commissions** | Each commission (Punjab, Bengal) had four judges: two nominated by Congress, two by the Muslim League. There was no casting mechanism except Radcliffe. Deadlock was structurally guaranteed; the "commission" was procedural theatre for a decision by one man. |
| **The terms of reference** | He was told to demarcate "on the basis of ascertaining the contiguous majority areas of Muslims and non-Muslims" and to "take into account other factors" — with *"other factors"* left completely undefined. |
| **No enforcement power** | The commission had no mandate over population transfer, no troops, and no authority over the timing of publication. |

Radcliffe's own verdict on the exercise was that the time available made a better job
impossible. He refused his fee, destroyed his working papers, and never returned to India.

**The mistakes below are the ones that remain after these constraints are subtracted out.**

---

## 2. The mistakes

### 2.1 Data failures

**M1 — Reliance on the 1941 census, uncorrected.**
The 1941 census was taken under wartime conditions and was widely understood *at the time* to
be communally inflated: both Congress and the League had incentives to over-report their
constituencies, and enumeration quality was poor. This was not hindsight. It was argued in
front of Radcliffe — Congress submitted that the census's unreliability meant "other factors"
should be weighted heavily; the League submitted that the figures were sound and "other
factors" could be ignored.

Radcliffe never resolved this dispute. He neither adopted a correction methodology nor
formally rejected one. He used the raw figures where they suited a conclusion and reached
past them where they did not. **The failure is not using bad data — he had no better data.
It is failing to state a consistent rule for how much weight bad data would carry.**

**M2 — Inconsistent administrative unit.**
The mandate said "contiguous majority areas," not "contiguous majority *districts*." Radcliffe
worked at tehsil level in Punjab and thana level in Bengal — defensible — but the underlying
demographic data was not uniformly reliable at that granularity, and he did not descend below
it even where a tehsil was internally split at village level. Given no time for field survey,
this was near-unavoidable, but it left a systematic error band he never quantified.

**M3 — No ground survey.**
No field visits, no verification of the maps against terrain. The line was drawn from maps and
written memoranda in Delhi. Portions of it were later found to be geographically incoherent —
running through buildings, cutting villages from their fields and wells.

### 2.2 Process failures

**M4 — The award was written without adequate published reasoning.**
This is the most consequential procedural error and it is widely overstated in both
directions. Radcliffe's award text was *not* silent — he explicitly justified the Gurdaspur
allocation by reference to the integrity of the Upper Bari Doab Canal and the Madhopur
headworks, and he expressly proposed joint control at Hussainiwala. But the reasoning is thin,
applied unevenly, and absent entirely at the points of greatest controversy. An award that
reallocates 88 million people needs to be auditable. This one is not.

**M5 — Destruction of the working papers.**
Radcliffe burned his notes and drafts before leaving India. Combined with M4, this made the
award permanently unfalsifiable. Every accusation of bad faith made since 1947 — in all
directions — draws its oxygen from the fact that no one can check. Whatever his motive
(protecting himself, protecting Mountbatten, or simple exhaustion), the effect was to convert
a contestable technical decision into a permanent grievance.

**M6 — Publication was withheld until 17 August, two days after independence.**
The award was substantially complete around 12–13 August. Mountbatten held it back so that it
would not sour the independence ceremonies. The decision was Mountbatten's, not Radcliffe's —
but Radcliffe acquiesced, and the consequences were the worst of the entire partition:

- On 14–15 August, millions of people in the contested belt **did not know which country they
  were in**.
- Civil administration, police, and army units in mixed districts had no lawful orders.
- The Punjab Boundary Force — roughly 50,000 men for a belt of ~14 districts and ~14 million
  people — was inadequate on its face, and was dissolved on 1 September, during the peak of
  the killing.

**Of every decision made in 1947, this delay has the strongest causal claim on the death
toll.** The line's placement determined who *had* to move; the secrecy determined that they
moved late, in panic, and without protection.

**M7 — Susceptibility to viceregal influence.**
See M9. The evidentiary record here is contested but not empty.

### 2.3 Errors in the line itself — Punjab

**M8 — Gurdaspur.**
Gurdaspur district was **50.2% Muslim** overall (pop. ~757,000; Sikhs ~27%, Hindus ~21%).
Tehsil-level Muslim shares in 1941:

| Tehsil | Muslim % | Radcliffe's award |
|---|---|---|
| Batala | ~55% | **India** |
| Gurdaspur | ~52% | **India** |
| Shakargarh | ~51% | Pakistan |
| Pathankot | ~40% | India |

Three of four tehsils held Muslim majorities; Radcliffe awarded two of those three to India.
His stated ground was canal integrity (Madhopur headworks, UBDC command). The consequence,
whether or not it was the intent, is that **India received its only land access to Jammu and
Kashmir**. Without the Gurdaspur–Pathankot corridor there is no road from India to Kashmir,
and the accession of October 1947 is militarily unenforceable.

This is the single most contested segment of the entire line. Pakistani historiography treats
it as the decisive act of bad faith; Indian and British accounts defend it on canal-integrity
and contiguity grounds (Amritsar, a Sikh–Hindu-majority district and the site of the Golden
Temple, would otherwise be nearly enclaved). **Both readings survive the evidence.** What does
not survive is the claim that the allocation followed the stated demographic rule — it plainly
did not.

**M9 — Ferozepur and Zira: the reversal.**
A draft line shown to Governor Jenkins in early August placed the Muslim-majority tehsils of
**Ferozepur (~55% Muslim)** and **Zira (~65% Muslim)** in Pakistan. Radcliffe showed this draft
to Mountbatten at a lunch on 9 August. Around 10–11 August, a message from the Viceroy's House
reached Jenkins reading **"Eliminate Salient."** The final award of 12 August moved both
tehsils to India, taking the Ferozepur headworks on the Sutlej with them.

Christopher Beaumont, Radcliffe's own private secretary, stated years later that Mountbatten
had improperly influenced the alteration.

This is the clearest documented instance of the award departing from its own criterion under
external pressure. Unlike Gurdaspur, there is no canal-integrity defence available here — the
Ferozepur headworks fed the Sutlej Valley canals commanding **Pakistani** territory
(Bahawalpur, Montgomery). The reversal was demographically wrong *and* hydrologically wrong in
the same stroke.

**M10 — Severing the canal system from its headworks.**
Punjab's irrigation network was engineered as a single integrated system; the partition line
cut it transversely rather than following it. Madhopur and Ferozepur headworks went to India
while substantial command areas they fed lay in Pakistan. Radcliffe was *aware* of this
problem — hence Madhopur reasoning, hence the joint-control proposal at Hussainiwala — which
makes the inconsistency worse, not better: he recognised the principle and then applied it to
some headworks and not others.

Result: the **April 1948 water cut-off**, when India stopped flows to Pakistani canals, and
twelve years of unresolved dispute until the **Indus Waters Treaty (1960)**. The treaty is,
functionally, the correction to M10 — arrived at thirteen years and one near-war too late.

**M11 — The Sikhs were written out of the mandate.**
The terms of reference recognised exactly two categories: "Muslims and non-Muslims." The Sikhs
were ~14–15% of Punjab, held a majority in **no district**, and owned a disproportionate share
of the canal-colony land in the west. Lumping them into "non-Muslims" made them statistically
invisible at every decision point.

Outcome: their holiest sites were split — **Nankana Sahib** (birthplace of Guru Nanak) and
**Panja Sahib** to Pakistan, **Amritsar** to India — and roughly two million Sikhs ended up on
the wrong side of the line. The Sikh community suffered the highest proportional casualty and
displacement rate of any group in the partition. Radcliffe did not write the terms of
reference, but he made no recorded attempt to have them amended, and "other factors" gave him
the latitude to weigh sacred geography had he chosen to.

### 2.4 Errors in the line itself — Bengal

**M12 — The Chittagong Hill Tracts.**
The CHT was **~97% non-Muslim** — Chakma, Marma, and other largely Buddhist and tribal
populations. Under a plain reading of the mandate it goes to India without argument. Radcliffe
awarded it to **Pakistan**, on the "other factors" ground that its only viable access was
through Chittagong port, which was itself Muslim-majority.

The CHT raised Indian flags on 15 August 1947; Pakistani troops removed them. The decision
produced decades of insurgency and demographic displacement that continue to generate conflict.

**The CHT is the strongest single piece of evidence that the award was arbitrary rather than
principled** — because it is the mirror image of Gurdaspur. In Gurdaspur, a Muslim-majority
district was given to India on "other factors." In the CHT, a 97% non-Muslim district was given
to Pakistan on "other factors." The two decisions cannot be derived from any common rule.

**M13 — The Khulna / Murshidabad trade.**
**Murshidabad (~56% Muslim)** went to India, because it commands the Bhagirathi–Hooghly flow
that keeps Calcutta port navigable. **Khulna (~51% Hindu)** went to Pakistan, in what is
transparently a compensating exchange. Both allocations invert the stated criterion, in
opposite directions, for reasons of infrastructure and balance. The trade may well have been
the pragmatic answer; presenting it as the output of a demographic commission was not honest.

**M14 — Economic amputation of East Bengal.**
East Bengal produced the overwhelming majority of the world's raw jute. **Essentially every
jute mill was in West Bengal, around Calcutta.** The rail network radiated from Calcutta;
Chittagong was an underdeveloped alternative port. The award handed East Pakistan the crop and
none of the processing, transport, or export capacity.

Calcutta's allocation to India was demographically correct and probably unavoidable. But the
commission made **no provision whatever** — no transitional access regime, no port arrangement,
no joint infrastructure body — for the economic organism it was bisecting. East Pakistan's
structural economic subordination begins here, and runs forward to 1971.

**M15 — The enclaves left unresolved.**
The Cooch Behar–Rangpur belt contained **197 enclaves** (*chhitmahals*), including
counter-enclaves and the world's only counter-counter-enclave, Dahala Khagrabari. These derived
from pre-colonial princely landholdings. The commission simply did not address them.

Roughly 50,000 people were left effectively stateless — without lawful access to police,
schools, courts, or hospitals — for **sixty-eight years**, until the Land Boundary Agreement of
2015.

### 2.5 The omission that dominates all others

**M16 — No population transfer plan, and an explicit assumption that none was needed.**
Radcliffe's mandate excluded population transfer, and no other body picked it up. Radcliffe
himself proceeded on the assumption that populations would **stay where they were** — the
boundary was not intended to trigger migration.

That assumption was catastrophically wrong. The result was **14–18 million displaced** and a
death toll estimated between **200,000 and 2 million**.

No boundary commission should be permitted to draw a communal line while formally disclaiming
responsibility for what the line does to the people on the wrong side of it. That is a defect
in the institutional design — but Radcliffe, having seen the demographic maps, was better
placed than anyone alive to know the assumption was false, and left no record of having said
so.

## 3. Severity ranking

Ranked by casualties and durable consequence, not by how much attention each receives:

| Rank | Mistake | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **M6** — publication withheld to 17 August | Primary driver of the massacre toll |
| 2 | **M16** — no transfer plan, wrong standing assumption | 14–18M displaced without protection |
| 3 | **M10** — canal system severed from headworks | 1948 water crisis; 13-year dispute; IWT |
| 4 | **M8** — Gurdaspur | Kashmir land access; three subsequent wars |
| 5 | **M12** — Chittagong Hill Tracts | Decades of insurgency; proof of arbitrariness |
| 6 | **M11** — Sikhs excluded from the mandate | Highest proportional casualty rate |
| 7 | **M14** — East Bengal's economic amputation | Structural subordination → 1971 |
| 8 | **M9** — Ferozepur/Zira reversal | Clearest documented external interference |
| 9 | **M4 / M5** — thin reasoning, burnt papers | Permanent unfalsifiability of the record |
| 10 | **M15** — enclaves | 50,000 people stateless for 68 years |

Note that **the two worst items are not about where the line was drawn.** This is the central
finding of the analysis and §4 returns to it.

## 4. The optimal line

### 4.1 Criteria

"Optimal" is meaningless without an objective function. The following are ranked, and the
ranking is the argument:

1. **Demographic fidelity** — minimise persons placed under a majority not their own.
2. **Systemic integrity** — do not sever an irrigation system from its headworks, a crop from
   its processing, or a port from its hinterland.
3. **Sacred geography** — where a community's holiest sites can be kept with that community at
   acceptable demographic cost, do so.
4. **Contiguity and administrability** — no enclaves, no salients, follow real terrain.
5. **Defensibility** — the line should not create an obvious future casus belli.

These conflict. In Punjab, criteria 1 and 2 are close to **orthogonal**: the demography runs
north–south, the rivers and canal commands run northeast–southwest. No line satisfies both.
This is the fact that makes Punjab genuinely hard, and it is why the honest answer in §4.4 is
not a line at all.

### 4.2 The optimal Punjab line

Working north to south:

**Segment 1 — Kashmir foothills to the Ravi.** Shakargarh tehsil to Pakistan, boundary on the
Ravi. *Same as Radcliffe.* Correct as drawn.

**Segment 2 — Gurdaspur (the hard case).** Strict demography awards Batala (~55%), Gurdaspur
(~52%), and Shakargarh (~51%) to Pakistan, leaving Pathankot (~40% Muslim) with India,
contiguous via Kangra.

The geography forbids splitting the difference. The tehsil chain runs Amritsar → Batala →
Gurdaspur → Pathankot. Ceding **either** Batala or Gurdaspur tehsil severs everything north of
it from Amritsar. There is no intermediate option.

**Verdict: retain Radcliffe's allocation, but on stated and consistent grounds.** The
justification is criterion 2 (Madhopur headworks with the UBDC command) plus criterion 4
(Amritsar is otherwise near-enclaved) plus criterion 3 (the Golden Temple). The cost is roughly
**350,000–400,000 Muslims** placed in India against the demographic rule, and it should have
been stated in exactly those terms in the award.

This is the segment where I depart least from Radcliffe and criticise him most: **the outcome
was defensible and the silence was not.** Had the award said plainly *"I am awarding two
Muslim-majority tehsils to India because the canal command and Amritsar's contiguity require
it, at a cost of ~375,000 people,"* Gurdaspur would be a hard trade-off in the historical
record instead of a seventy-nine-year accusation.

**Segment 3 — Ferozepur and Zira: reverse the alteration. Both to Pakistan.**

This is the **single clearest available improvement to the line**, and the only segment where
demography and hydrology point the same way:

- Demographically correct: Ferozepur ~55% Muslim, Zira ~65% Muslim.
- Hydrologically correct: the Ferozepur headworks are placed with the Sutlej Valley canals they
  actually command, in Bahawalpur and Montgomery.
- Geographically correct: both tehsils are contiguous with West Punjab; the "salient"
  Mountbatten ordered eliminated was the *natural* shape of the line.
- Removes the award's most documented instance of external interference.

**Cost:** India loses the Ferozepur headworks and with them assured control of the Bikaner
canal supply into Rajasthan. This is a real loss and must be paid for — in §4.4's water
instrument, by a guaranteed-delivery obligation rather than by ownership of the works. That is
precisely the trade the Indus Waters Treaty eventually made in 1960.

**Segment 4 — Lahore to the southern boundary.** Lahore district (~61% Muslim) to Pakistan;
Amritsar (~46.5% Muslim, Sikh–Hindu majority) to India. *Same as Radcliffe.* Correct, and
correct for the stated reason. Lahore's Hindu and Sikh residents owned a large share of the
city's property, but the mandate counted people, not deeds, and on that rule the answer is not
close.

**Segment 5 — Jullundur, Ludhiana, Ambala division.** Unchanged. Clear non-Muslim majorities;
no serious question arises.

**Sikh sacred sites.** No line reachable from the demographic map puts Nankana Sahib in India —
it lies deep in Sheikhupura district, in overwhelmingly Muslim territory, ~65 km west of Lahore.
Any corridor to it would be a 65 km salient through hostile demography: indefensible,
unadministrable, and a guaranteed flashpoint. **The correct instrument is not a line but a
treaty** — guaranteed pilgrimage access with extraterritorial protection for the shrine
complexes, negotiated *before* the award rather than improvised for decades afterward. This is
the honest answer to M11, and it concedes that M11 could not have been fully solved by
cartography.

### 4.3 The optimal Bengal line

Bengal is where the line itself was genuinely more wrong, and where more is recoverable:

**Change 1 — Chittagong Hill Tracts to India.** A 97% non-Muslim population cannot be awarded
to Pakistan under a mandate about communal majorities without voiding the mandate. If the
access problem through Chittagong port is judged decisive, the correct answer is a **guaranteed
transit corridor or a special autonomous status**, not silent reassignment of a whole people.
This is the largest single correctable error in the entire award.

**Change 2 — Khulna and Murshidabad: apply the rule, then negotiate the infrastructure.**
Murshidabad (~56% Muslim) to Pakistan and Khulna (~51% Hindu) to India is what the criterion
requires.

But criterion 2 genuinely bites here: Murshidabad commands the Bhagirathi headwaters that keep
Calcutta port navigable, and Calcutta is indisputably Indian. So the defensible resolution is
**Radcliffe's outcome reached honestly**: award Murshidabad to India explicitly as an
infrastructure exception, state the reason, and *do not* pay for it by trading Khulna's Hindu
majority to Pakistan as silent compensation. Khulna should have gone to India on the rule. A
commission may make exceptions; it may not make barter and call it demography.

**Change 3 — Enclaves resolved in the award itself.** The 197 chhitmahals were visible on the
maps in 1947. A schedule of territorial exchange — substantially the 2015 Land Boundary
Agreement, which is what eventually happened — was available at the time and would have cost
nothing but attention. **68 years of statelessness for ~50,000 people was pure omission.**

**Change 4 — Calcutta to India, with a jute and port instrument attached.** The allocation is
right; the absence of any transitional arrangement is not. A time-limited raw-jute access and
port-transit agreement was the obvious accompaniment and was never proposed.

### 4.4 The finding that matters

Having drawn the optimal line, the honest conclusion has to be stated plainly:

**The line was not the main problem.**

Even a perfectly drawn boundary leaves an irreducible floor of roughly **10–15% of the affected
population** on the "wrong" side — approximately **4–5 million people in Punjab alone**, and
substantially more in Bengal. Punjab and Bengal were interdigitated at the village level. There
is no curve on that map that separates these populations. My corrections above improve
demographic fidelity by perhaps **1.5–2 percentage points** over Radcliffe's line.

Against that: publishing the same award on **1 July 1947**, six weeks before the transfer of
power, with

1. the boundary known in advance so people could move deliberately rather than in panic,
2. a standing water treaty (the 1960 Indus Waters Treaty, thirteen years early),
3. a planned, escorted, resourced population transfer instead of a disclaimed one,
4. a boundary force sized for 14 million people rather than 50,000 men,

would plausibly have prevented **the large majority of the deaths** — with the line drawn
exactly where Radcliffe drew it.

**The optimal partition line is roughly Radcliffe's line, corrected at Ferozepur–Zira and the
Chittagong Hill Tracts, published six weeks early, with a water treaty and a transfer plan
attached.** The cartography was ~85% of the achievable ceiling. The timing, the secrecy, and
the disclaimed responsibility for consequences are where the catastrophe actually lives — and
those were the parts nobody was asked to optimise.
