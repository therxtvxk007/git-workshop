# Panchavati Hills, Pune: A Search of the Classical and Mythological Record

**Question asked:** find all historical mentions of "Panchavati Hills" in Pune in classical or
mythological Indian literature.

**Short answer:** There are none. No classical Sanskrit, Prakrit, Puranic, epic, or early
vernacular text places a Panchavati — hill, grove, or settlement — in the Pune region. The
Panchavati of Indian literature is unambiguously located on the **Godavari**, and Pune is not in
the Godavari basin. The "Panchavati" in Pune is a modern residential locality in Pashan, and the
name is an instance of a widely reproduced sacred-grove name, not an epic survival.

This document sets out (1) what the Pune Panchavati actually is, (2) the complete classical
attestation record for the real Panchavati, (3) where scholars genuinely disagree about its
location and why that disagreement does not help Pune, (4) the mechanism by which the name
reached Pune anyway, and (5) what Pune's hills *are* attested as in classical, Puranic, and
epigraphic literature — which is where the real material is.

---

## 1. What "Panchavati Hill, Pune" actually is

Panchawati (also spelled Panchavati) is a locality in the **Pashan** area of western Pune, PIN
411008, lying between Bhageerath, Pashan, and Gokhale Nagar, near Pashan Lake. There is a
"Panchavati Hill Walking Trail" on the slope above it. The hill is part of the western hill
chain of Pune — the Vetal Hill / Baner–Pashan ridge system — and is documented today mainly in
biodiversity surveys (it appears as a butterfly-recording site) and property listings.

It is a twentieth-century residential development. It has no pre-modern documentary record: it
does not appear in Puranic tirtha lists, in inscriptions, in the Bombay/Maharashtra Gazetteer
material on ancient Poona, or in any Ramayana recension. Nothing about the hill is old except
the basalt.

## 2. The classical attestation record for Panchavati

Every attestation below gives the same geography. This is the point: the corpus is not vague
about where Panchavati is.

### 2.1 Valmiki Ramayana, Aranya Kanda — the foundational text

Panchavati enters literature here, and the Aranya Kanda supplies its coordinates directly.

| Passage | Content |
|---|---|
| Sarga 11–13 | Rama, Sita and Lakshmana reach the ashram of **Agastya** in Dandakaranya. |
| Sarga 13 | Agastya, foreseeing what is to come, directs Rama to Panchavati: a prosperous place **two yojanas** away, abundant in tubers, fruits, water and deer, **on the bank of the Godavari**. |
| Sarga 14 | En route, the meeting with the vulture-king **Jatayu**. |
| Sarga 15 | Arrival. Rama: "this region with its flowered forests is Panchavati." He asks Lakshmana to choose a site **near the Godavari**. |
| Sarga 16 | Lakshmana builds the **parnashala** (leaf-hut); the house-entering rite; the celebrated description of the Godavari in winter. |
| Sarga 17–18 | **Shurpanakha** approaches Rama at Panchavati; her disfigurement. |
| Sarga 19–30 | The **Khara–Dushana** war in **Janasthana**. |
| Sarga 42–47 | The golden deer; **Sita's abduction** from the Panchavati hermitage. |

The locational markers used throughout are exactly three: **Godavari**, **Janasthana**,
**Dandakaranya**. No hill range is named as Panchavati's setting. No river of the Pune region —
Mula, Mutha, Pavana, Indrayani, Bhima — appears anywhere in the episode.

### 2.2 Kalidasa, *Raghuvamsha*, Canto 13 (c. 4th–5th century CE)

The earliest major independent *kavya* attestation. Canto 13 is given wholly to Rama's return
flight to Ayodhya aboard the **Pushpaka**, with Rama pointing out to Sita the scenes of their
exile below. At **13.35** he recalls Panchavati directly:

> at Panchavati, near the Godavari, returning from the hunt, the breeze off the river's ripples
> carrying away my fatigue, I slept with my head resting on your lap in the cane groves — I
> remember it now.

Kalidasa also names **Janasthana** in the same sequence. A poet writing centuries after the epic,
with no stake in the geography, independently places Panchavati on the Godavari.

### 2.3 Bhavabhuti, *Uttararamacharita*, Act II (c. 700–740 CE)

Act II is titled *Sita in the Dandaka Forest*. Rama returns to Panchavati and meets **Vasanti**,
the presiding forest deity — one of the most admired scenes in Sanskrit drama. Bhavabhuti's
Dandaka forest extends southward to **Janasthana on the Godavari**.

This is the single most significant negative datum for the Pune hypothesis. Bhavabhuti was
himself a **Deccan poet**, from Vidarbha — the closest any major classical dramatist stands to
the Pune region. Writing about Panchavati at length, with Deccan geography in front of him, he
places it on the Godavari.

### 2.4 The Puranic layer

Panchavati appears in Puranic literature as a **tirtha on the southern bank of the Godavari**,
embedded in the older Godavari cycle: the **Gautama–Ahalya** narrative of the drought and the
descent of the river, which is why the Godavari is worshipped as **Dakshina Ganga** ("the Ganga
of the South"). The Puranic frame is therefore *river-cult* geography — Panchavati is a
riverside site in a river's sacred topography, not a hill site.

### 2.5 Later Ramayana traditions

The Godavari location is carried unchanged through the *Adhyatma Ramayana*, Tulsidas's
*Ramcharitmanas* (Aranya Kand), Kamban's Tamil *Ramavataram*, and — decisively for this question
— **Eknath's *Bhavartha Ramayana*** (16th century), a **Marathi** retelling produced within
Maharashtra itself. A Marathi poet in the Deccan had every opportunity to localise Panchavati
nearer home. He did not.

### 2.6 Etymology

*Pañcavaṭī* = *pañca* ("five") + *vaṭa* ("banyan"): the grove of five banyan trees. The Puranic
gloss is that five *vaṭavṛkṣas* stood there in a circle. This etymological transparency matters
for §4 — the name describes a **type of grove**, which is precisely why it can be reproduced
anywhere.

## 3. Where scholars actually disagree

There is a genuine dispute about Panchavati's location, and it is worth stating accurately
because it still does not admit Pune.

- **Nashik (mainstream and traditional).** Modern Nashik is identified with Panchavati in
  Janasthana; the site sits on the Godavari and carries the associated shrines (Sita Gufa, Ram
  Kund, the five banyans). Agastya's ashram, at the required two yojanas, is identified with
  **Agastyapuri** (about 24 miles SE of Nashik) or **Akolha** (east of Nashik). The traditional
  yuga-sequence of names for the site runs Padmanagar → Trikantak → Janasthana → Nashik.
- **Bhadrachalam (the minority position).** Some scholars — drawing on the Telugu *Andhra Valmiki
  Ramayana* tradition of Vasudasa Swamy — argue that the Panchavati and Parnasala of the text
  fit the **Godavari at Bhadrachalam**, far downstream in present-day Telangana, better than the
  Nashik stretch.

Both candidates are **on the Godavari**, roughly 800 km apart along the same river, and the
argument between them is entirely an argument about *which reach of the Godavari*. Pune is not a
candidate in this debate and never has been, for a reason that is decisive and purely
geographical:

> **Pune is not in the Godavari basin at all.** Pune sits in the **Bhima basin** — the Mula,
> Mutha, Pavana and Indrayani feed the Bhima, which joins the **Krishna**. The Godavari
> watershed lies north of the Pune district boundary. The single most repeated marker for
> Panchavati in every text that mentions it is a river that does not flow near Pune.

## 4. So why is there a Panchavati in Pune?

Because "Panchavati" is not only a proper noun. It is a **reproducible ritual plantation type**.

A Panchavati is a grove of five sacred trees planted in five prescribed directions — commonly
*vata* (banyan, *Ficus benghalensis*), *ashvattha* (peepal, *Ficus religiosa*), *bilva* (*Aegle
marmelos*), *amalaki* (*Emblica officinalis*), and *ashoka* (*Saraca indica*), with neem
substituting in some lists. The merit of tree-planting is set out in the *Padma Purana*; the
five-direction arrangement is associated with the *Skanda Purana* and the *Vrikshayurveda*
tradition (which is itself referenced across the Matsya, Varaha, Padma and Agni Puranas).

Because it is a *format*, people have deliberately built new Panchavatis for centuries:

- **Dakshineswar, Kolkata.** Five trees — banyan, peepal, neem, amalaki and bilva — were planted
  under **Sri Ramakrishna's** direction, creating a new Panchavati where he performed his
  Advaitic sadhana under Totapuri (initiation 1864; the sadhana of 1865). Explicitly a *new*
  Panchavati, consecrated as such.
- **New Delhi.** The Prime Minister's residence complex at 7, Lok Kalyan Marg is officially named
  **Panchavati** — five bungalows on about 12 acres, built in the 1980s.
- **Ahmedabad.** A Panchavati locality and municipal park near Ellisbridge.

Pune's Panchavati belongs to this class. It is a modern locality bearing a common, deliberately
reproduced sacred-grove name — the same phenomenon as Delhi's and Ahmedabad's, not a fragment of
epic geography.

**One local resonance worth flagging, with a caution.** The **Ramnadi** ("Rama's river") rises in
the Sahyadri foothills west of Pune and runs about 19 km through Bhukum, Bhugaon, Bavdhan,
Pashan and Baner to meet the Mula at Aundh — passing directly by the Panchavati locality. A
Rama-named stream beside a Panchavati-named hill is suggestive. But there is **no documented
Ramayana tradition** attached to Pune's Ramnadi; its own recorded history is a modern urban-stream
story (it feeds British-era Pashan Lake and is now heavily polluted). Treat the pairing as a
plausible naming resonance, not as evidence.

## 5. What Pune's hills *are* attested as

This is the material the question was probably reaching for. Pune district has a deep and
genuinely documented sacred and epigraphic geography — it simply is not a Ramayana geography.

### 5.1 Epigraphy — the hardest evidence

- **Naneghat inscription of Queen Naganika** (c. 90 BCE – 30 CE), on the Naneghat pass about 27
  km NW of **Junnar**, Pune district. Brahmi script, Prakrit language. It is the **earliest
  epigraphic record from western India**, and it opens with invocations to Vedic and Puranic
  deities. Naneghat itself (*nane* = coin, *ghat* = pass) was a tollbooth on the trade route
  linking the Konkan ports to the Deccan.
- **Karla, Bhaja and Bedse caves**, Maval taluka, Pune district, from the 1st century BCE. Karla
  carries **22 inscriptions**, mostly Prakrit in Brahmi, spanning the 2nd century BCE to the 2nd
  century CE, naming donors and the towns they came from. Bedse records donations by merchants
  such as Maha Sethi Bhuta and Mitra Sethi.
- **Junnar** (ancient *Jirnanagara*) was the Satavahana vice-capital to Paithan's capital. The
  *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea* describes Roman goods landing at **Kalyan** and **Sopara** and
  moving inland through this corridor.

### 5.2 Pune-district hills that genuinely appear in Sanskrit religious texts

- **Bhimashankar** (Sahyadri, Pune district, c. 3,250 ft). The sixth **Jyotirlinga**. The *Shiva
  Purana* places Bhimashankar on the **Sahya mountain**: Shiva destroys the asura **Tripurasura**
  here, and his perspiration from the battle forms the **Bhima river**, which rises on these
  slopes. This is a Pune-district hill named in a major Purana.
- **Lenyadri**, Junnar. About 30 Buddhist rock-cut caves; the **Girijatmaj** Ashtavinayak shrine
  occupies one of them — the only Ashtavinayak temple inside a mountain cave. The name
  **Lenyadri** appears in the *Ganesha Purana* and its sthala-purana.
- **Ashtavinayak circuit.** Five of the eight shrines are in Pune district — Mayureshwar
  (Morgaon), Chintamani (Theur), Mahaganapati (Ranjangaon), Vighneshwar (Ozar), Girijatmaj
  (Lenyadri) — with two in Raigad and one in Ahmednagar. The circuit's textual footing is the
  *Ganesha Purana* / *Mudgala Purana* tradition.
- **Jejuri**, Pune district — the hill shrine of **Khandoba / Malhari Martand**, and his victory
  over the demons **Mani and Malla**. The principal written source is the *Malhari Mahatmya*,
  which claims to be the *Kshetra-kanda* of the **Brahmanda Purana** — though it is **not** found
  in standard editions of that Purana. R. C. Dhere and G.-D. Sontheimer date the Sanskrit text to
  roughly **1460–1510 CE**; a Marathi version by Siddhapal Kesasri follows in **1585**.
- **The Sahyadri range as a whole** — the *Sahyadrikhanda*, a Sanskrit text claiming to belong to
  the *Skanda Purana* and offering a mythological, historical and geographical account of western
  India. It is a composite of materials dating roughly from the 5th to the 13th centuries and
  existed by the 13th, since Hemadri's *Chaturvarga-chintamani* quotes it. Its authority is
  contested: it glorifies the Shenvis and disparages the Chitpavans and Karhades, and Chitpavan
  scholars long denounced it as a fabrication. Use it with that caveat.

### 5.3 The earliest documentary name of the Pune region

Not a hill, but the point at which "Pune" enters the written record: two **Rashtrakuta copper
plates of Krishna I**, dated **758 and 768 CE**, which call the region **Punya-vishaya** and
**Punaka-vishaya** respectively. A related charter grants the village of *Kumarigram* (modern
Koregaon), situating it with hills to the south and the **Mula river** to the north. (Sources
vary in the details — one account gives 786 CE and a district named *Punnat* — so the precise
plate readings are worth checking against the epigraphic corpus.)

### 5.4 Named hills in Pune city, and how old they really are

- **Parvati Hill** — Pune city's best-known hill and the oldest surviving heritage structure in
  the city. The Devdeveshwar temple was completed under **Balaji Baji Rao (Nanasaheb Peshwa) in
  1749**, with a gold pinnacle added in 1760, reportedly fulfilling a vow made by his mother
  Kashibai. Peshwa-era, roughly 275 years old — not ancient.
- **Pataleshwar** (Shivajinagar) — a basalt rock-cut cave temple to Shiva, carved in the
  **8th century** under the Rashtrakutas (c. 750 CE). The oldest monument in Pune city proper.
- **Sinhagad**, formerly **Kondhana** — with Satavahana-period cisterns and carvings on the hill,
  and a firm documentary appearance in **1328**, when Muhammad bin Tughluq's forces took it from
  the Koli chieftain Nag Naik after an eight-month siege.
- **Vetal Hill / Vetal Tekdi** — the ridge system that the Pashan "Panchavati Hill" belongs to.
  Named for the **Vetala**, the spirit of folklore (as in the *Vetala-panchavimshati*). Folklore,
  not Puranic geography.

## 6. Method, and what would change the answer

What was checked: the Valmiki Ramayana Aranya Kanda episode in full and its locational markers;
the independent classical attestations (Kalidasa, Bhavabhuti); the Puranic Godavari cycle; later
Ramayana recensions including the Marathi *Bhavartha Ramayana*; the scholarly literature on
Panchavati's identification; the epigraphic and Puranic record for Pune district; and the
etymology and reproduction of the Panchavati grove-type.

Three findings would overturn the conclusion, and none of them exists:

1. A text placing Panchavati on a **Bhima-basin river** (Mula, Mutha, Pavana, Indrayani) rather
   than the Godavari. Every text says Godavari.
2. A pre-modern **inscription, copper plate, or land grant** from the Pune region using the name
   Panchavati. The Pune-region charters name Punya/Punaka-vishaya, Kumarigram, and the Mula.
3. A **local Rama tradition** in Pune district comparable to Nashik's Sita Gufa and Ram Kund.
   Pune district's hill traditions are Shaiva (Bhimashankar), Ganapatya (Lenyadri, the
   Ashtavinayak circuit), and folk-Shaiva (Khandoba at Jejuri) — not Ramaite.

The honest conclusion is that the Pune "Panchavati" is a modern name of a common and well-
understood kind, and that the search for classical mentions of it returns nothing — while the
search for classical mentions of Pune's *actual* hills returns a great deal.
