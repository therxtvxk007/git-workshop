# What Is a Nation?

**A synthesis across political science, history, anthropology, sociology, law, economics, cognitive science, and genomics — evidence through August 2026.**

---

## 1. The definition

**Short form**

> A **nation** is a large, named human population whose members imagine themselves as a single bounded people, continuous across generations and attached to a homeland, and who on that basis claim the right to govern themselves.

**Compressed to a glossary line**

> A nation is a population that believes it is one people, entitled to rule itself.

**Full analytic form**

> A **nation** is a named human population, too large for its members to know one another, that (i) is *bounded* by a working membership rule distinguishing members from non-members; (ii) is *imagined as continuous* — a single people persisting backward into an origin story and forward into an obligated future; (iii) is *anchored to a homeland*, a territory held to be the group's by right, whether or not the group currently occupies or controls it; (iv) *claims political self-rule* on the strength of (i)–(iii), this claim being what makes the population a nation rather than merely a culture, a religion, or an ethnic group; and (v) is *reproduced institutionally* — through schooling, language policy, media, census categories, ritual, currency, borders, and conscription — rather than by any shared biological, linguistic, or confessional trait.

Nationhood so defined is **gradable, time-indexed, and two-sided**: a population can be more or less a nation, can become one and cease to be one, and its nationhood depends both on internal uptake and on external recognition. There is no fact of the matter independent of those.

## 2. Why the traditional definitions fail

| Definition | Core claim | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| **Stalin (1913)** | Common language + territory + economic life + psychological make-up — *all four required* | Conjunctive checklists exclude Switzerland (four languages), pre-1948 Jewry (no territory), and nearly every post-colonial state at independence (no common economic life). One counterexample kills a conjunction. |
| **Renan (1882)** | "A daily plebiscite" — a legacy of memories plus present consent, sustained by shared *forgetting* | Right about voluntarism and about forgetting being constitutive, but far too thin to distinguish a nation from any voluntary association. |
| **Weber (*Economy and Society*)** | A community of sentiment that "would adequately manifest itself in a state of its own" | Closest of the classics — puts the *sovereignty claim* at the center and correctly treats objective markers as unreliable — but leaves "sentiment" undefined. |
| **Gellner (1983)** | Nationalism is the principle that political and national units should be congruent; nations are a by-product of industrialism's need for standardized high culture | Functionalist. Explains why *some* nation form appears under industrialism, not which nations form or why pre-industrial cases exist. |
| **Anderson (1983)** | "An imagined political community — imagined as both inherently limited and sovereign" | The best single sentence ever written on this, and the base of the definition above. Weaknesses: "imagined" is chronically misread as "imaginary"; it under-distinguishes a nation from other imagined limited communities; it over-weights print capitalism as cause. |
| **A. D. Smith (1991)** | "A named human population sharing an historic territory, common myths and historical memories, a mass, public culture, a common economy and common legal rights and duties" | A checklist again — but offered as an *ideal type*, which rescues it by allowing degrees. Still conflates the nation's typical outputs with what makes it one. |
| **Brubaker (1996, 2004)** | Stop asking "what is a nation." A nation is not a thing but an institutionalized *category of practice*; study "nationness" as a contingent event | The single most important corrective in the field — and a dodge. Comparative work needs a definition to compare cases; "it happens" is not one. |

The common failure is **mistaking manifestations for essence**. Language, ancestry myth, shared economy, and territorial control are things nations *typically acquire*, mostly because sovereignty produces them (a state standardizes a language; it does not require one to exist). Building them into the definition inverts the causal arrow.

## 3. What each field contributes

### Genomics — nations have no biological correlate

Human genetic variation is clinal and continuous. It does not partition into clusters matching national, ethnic, or racial categories; the National Academies' 2023 report on population descriptors and subsequent work on the geographic history of human ancestry (*Science*, 2024) both conclude that these descriptors track genomic variation poorly and should not be used as proxies for it. Where descent claims have been checked directly, mythical common ancestry appears above the clan level as a *unifying invention*, not a finding.

**Constraint imposed:** any descent content in a nation is *believed* descent. This is not a minor caveat — it means the "objective" side of the objective/subjective debate has no biological floor to stand on. Ancestry cannot be a criterion; only *beliefs about* ancestry can.

### Cognitive and evolutionary science — constructed, yet psychologically real

Recent work (a 2025 *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* treatment of coalitional psychology and nationalistic cultures; 2025 work on the neurocognitive foundations of nationalism) converges on a mechanism: nationhood recruits evolved machinery for coalition detection, kin-directed altruism, essentialist reasoning about social kinds, and collective memory. Empirically, belief in national *continuity* correlates with essentialist thinking about groups and with **identity fusion** — the visceral sense that the group is an extension of the self — and fusion tracks *cultural* continuity more strongly than historical continuity.

**Constraint imposed:** this dissolves the oldest false choice in the field. The nation is culturally constructed *and* feels primordial, because a constructed category can be a supernormal stimulus for kin psychology. "Socially constructed" never satisfied people who live inside a nation because it was heard as "not real." It is constructed and real in the way money is.

### History — the form is old; its dominance is modern

The strong modernist claim — that nations begin around 1789 or with industrialization — no longer holds as consensus. Grosby documents plausible nationhood in ancient Israel, Sasanian Iran, and medieval Scotland, Korea, Vietnam, and Sri Lanka; Gat argues that imagined community was achievable through spoken word and religious ritual long before print capitalism, and that pre-modern empires were not ethnically indifferent. *The Cambridge History of Nationhood and Nationalism* (Carmichael, D'Auria & Roshwald, eds., 2 vols.) leans toward this anti-modernist reading, treating kin-culture affinity as near-universal raw material; the 2025 symposium on it in *Nations and Nationalism* records how contested that lean remains.

**Constraint imposed:** modernity cannot be a *necessary condition*. What is distinctively modern is that the nation became the **default license for sovereignty** — the answer everyone is now required to give. UN membership going from 51 to 193 is that shift made administrative.

### International law — the state is defined; the nation deliberately is not

The **state** has a legal definition: permanent population, defined territory, government, and capacity to enter relations with other states (Montevideo Convention, 1933). The **nation** has none. Law instead uses "**peoples**" — the bearer of self-determination in Article 1 common to the ICCPR and ICESCR, and in UNDRIP (2007) Articles 3–4 for indigenous peoples — and leaves it *undefined on purpose*, with **self-identification** as a criterion (ILO Convention 169, Art. 1(2)).

**Constraint imposed:** the refusal to define is itself evidence. Nationhood in practice is *claimed and recognized*, not measured against a standard. Law encodes exactly the two-sided structure the definition needs. It also confirms nation ≠ state: hundreds of Native American and First Nations polities are legally *nations* without being states.

### Economics — the nation as fiscal-military technology

Alesina and Reich's nation-building model, and the more recent literature synthesized in the 2025 *Annual Review of Economics* treatment, describe nationhood as an elite investment: mass schooling, an official language, conscription, and broad public goods are purchased to lower the cost of taxation, mobilization, and redistribution. National identity forges an elite–mass consensus that lets revenue be collected and public goods delivered with less conflict; recent modeling adds that it makes state capacity worth paying for by converting rare crises into regular claims. Homogenization is bounded by unrest, electoral competition, and family transmission of identity.

**Constraint imposed:** "imagined community" is not the whole story — nationhood is also produced deliberately, at cost, by actors with a return in mind. Any definition that treats nations as purely spontaneous is missing the invoices.

### Empirical sociology — content varies; the form does not

The ISSP National Identity modules (1995, 2003, 2013, 2023) ask what makes someone a "true" member: birthplace, ancestry, religion, language, citizenship, respect for law. Across 30+ countries, people do **not** sort into civic and ethnic camps; most hold mixed bundles, and the mix varies by country and by person (Reeskens & Hooghe's 33-country analysis; Brubaker's and Shulman's critiques of the dichotomy). What the criteria reliably predict is **boundary permeability** — how open the nation is to newcomers — with ethnocultural conceptions most closed and civic most open.

**Constraint imposed:** never build a specific content into the definition. Content is the variable; the *form* — bounded peoplehood plus a sovereignty claim — is the constant. Civic vs. ethnic is a good measure of a nation's boundary, and a bad taxonomy of nations.

### Political theory, 2025–26 — separating appearance from essence

A recent line of argument (including "Defining the nation through the Kantian lens," *Frontiers in Political Science*, 2025) diagnoses the field's century-long definitional crisis as a systematic conflation of a nation's *observable appearances* with its *constitutive essence*, and proposes the cut directly: a nation is a population bound by a foundational myth of its **inherent right to sovereignty**; an ethnic group is a population bound by a myth of **common descent**. Cultural symbols and territorial claims are manifestations.

**Constraint imposed:** this is the sharpest available statement of the essence/appearance distinction and supplies the clean nation/ethnic-group boundary. The definition above accepts it, and adds back what a pure-myth formulation omits: the material and institutional apparatus economics and sociology document, and the recognition axis law documents.

### Digital and non-territorial claims — the territorial criterion, stress-tested

Diasporas — Jewish, Overseas Chinese, Sikh, Armenian — show that a unifying national narrative does not require occupying the homeland; the homeland is *referenced* rather than inhabited. Contemporary "network state" projects, Estonia's e-Residency, and micronation ventures push further, asserting functional over territorial sovereignty. All of them so far stall on the same two things: mass intergenerational uptake and external recognition. E-Residency confers no citizenship.

**Constraint imposed:** territory enters the definition as a *claimed homeland*, not as occupied ground — which is what lets exile and irredentist nations qualify. And the failure of network states isolates why: a sovereignty claim alone is not nationhood without a people that reproduces itself and others who acknowledge it.

## 4. Operationalizing it: five dimensions, each a matter of degree

Nationhood is not binary. Score any candidate population on five axes:

1. **Categorical salience** — is "we, the X" an available, used, taken-for-granted category in ordinary life? *(Brubaker's nationness; Billig's banal nationalism — the flag on the building, not the flag on the barricade.)*
2. **Boundedness** — is there a working membership rule, with edge cases people argue about rather than shrug at? *(ISSP boundary-permeability measures.)*
3. **Temporal depth** — is there a shared origin story and a felt obligation across generations? *(Renan's memory-and-forgetting; the continuity–essentialism–fusion findings.)*
4. **Sovereignty claim** — is self-rule asserted; by whom, how intensely, in what institutional form? *(Weber; the constitutive axis.)*
5. **Recognition** — internal uptake by the mass, not just elites; external acknowledgment by states and international bodies. *(Montevideo; UN practice; UNDRIP.)*

The interesting cases are the mismatches:

- **High 1–4, low 5** → *a nation without a state*: Kurds, Catalans, Palestinians, Tibetans, Taiwan (high internal, contested external).
- **High 5, low 1–3** → *a state without a nation*: most post-colonial states at independence. Hence *nation-building* as a literal program with a budget.
- **High on all five** → the canonical nation-state — historically rare, always partly a fiction, and never as homogeneous as its own story says.

## 5. Hard cases the definition must survive

| Case | Verdict | What it tests |
|---|---|---|
| **Switzerland** — 4 languages, 2 confessions | Nation | Kills every language- or ancestry-based definition. Passes on peoplehood + sovereignty claim. |
| **Jewish people pre-1948** | Nation (once the sovereignty claim was articulated) | Homeland as *reference*, not occupation; and shows nationhood can be activated by a claim within an older ethno-religious people. |
| **Roma** | Transnational ethnic people, not a nation | The discriminating case: high on boundedness, depth, and distinctness — but with no general claim to territorial self-rule. Exactly the cut the definition should make. |
| **Kurds** | Nation without a state | Isolates recognition as an independent axis. |
| **United States** | Nation | Creedal, immigrant-composed, minimal shared descent — proves content is variable and the form is what's constant. |
| **Nigeria, 1960** | State first, nationhood built after | Matches the economics literature exactly: a state buying a nation with schooling, language, and conscription. |
| **Cherokee Nation, First Nations** | Nations, not states | Nations nest inside states; legal usage matches the definition. UNDRIP's self-identification criterion is criterion (i) restated. |
| **Scotland, Catalonia** | Nations inside a state | Claim *intensity* varies over time; the 2014 and 2017 referenda are that variable made procedural. |
| **European Union** | Not a nation | Dense institutions, real budget, no imagined single peoplehood, no self-rule claim *as a people*. The best negative test available. |
| **Network states, Asgardia, Liberland** | Not nations | Sovereignty claim present; peoplehood, intergenerational continuity, and recognition absent. Self-ascription without uptake is noise. |
| **A religion, a football fandom, a class** | Not nations | All are imagined, bounded, ritual-rich communities. None claims sovereign self-rule over a homeland. This is the cut Anderson's sentence alone doesn't quite make. |
| **Han Chinese vs. Zhonghua minzu** | Ethnic core vs. constructed multi-ethnic nation | Nation and ethnic group come apart, deliberately and as policy. |

## 6. Disambiguation

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **State** | The legal-political apparatus holding a monopoly on legitimate force over a territory (Weber); in law, the Montevideo criteria. |
| **Country** | Informal; usually a state's territory. |
| **Nation** | The imagined sovereign people, as defined above. |
| **People** (legal) | The self-determination-bearing unit in international law — deliberately undefined, self-identification central. |
| **Ethnic group** | A population bound by a myth of common descent and cultural markers, *without* a necessary claim to self-rule. |
| **Nationality** | Ambiguous by design: legal membership (≈ citizenship) in most jurisdictions; ethno-national belonging in others, notably post-Soviet usage. Worth disambiguating whenever it appears. |
| **Nation-state** | The rare, aspirational congruence of one nation with one state — a normative program more often than a description. |

A note on etymology, because it does real damage: Latin *natio* means birth or breed, and at medieval universities "nations" were regional groupings of students. The descent-connotation in the word is a **fossil, not a criterion** — and it is precisely that fossil that keeps feeding essentialist misreadings of what nations are.

## 7. Where this lands theoretically

- **Against primordialism.** Nations are not natural kinds. Genomics settles that; no further argument is needed.
- **With modernism** on the *dominance* of the nation form, **against it** on *origins*. The historical record no longer supports the strong version.
- **With ethnosymbolism** that inherited symbols, myths, and memories are the raw material — while insisting they are raw material, not essence.
- **With Brubaker** that nationhood is an institutionalized category of practice — while recovering a usable definition anyway, because comparison requires one.
- **Adding the cognitive layer**, which is what lets the constructivist and primordialist intuitions both be right at once: constructed in origin, primordial in phenomenology.

**The one-line thesis.** A nation is not a kind of population; it is a *relationship a population has to the idea of ruling itself* — and everything else about it, including the traits its members are most certain define it, follows from that relationship rather than producing it.
