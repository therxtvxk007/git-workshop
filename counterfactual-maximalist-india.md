# The Geographically Sane Frontier: A Counterfactual Boundary Under a Bose Government

*Companion to `radcliffe-partition-analysis.md`. That document analyses what actually happened.
This one changes three premises and redraws the map.*

> **This is explicit alternate history.** None of the borders below existed. The premises
> require a man who died in August 1945 to have lived and taken power, which is itself the
> weakest link in the chain (§6.4).

---

## 1. The premises, and what they actually change

| # | Premise | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **P1** | Government is hypernationalist, under Subhas Chandra Bose | **No partition.** See §2. |
| **P2** | The state has territorial appetite and will annex and hold like the PRC did in Tibet and Xinjiang | The frontier is set by terrain and reach, not by consent |
| **P3** | Burma was never separated from India | The Government of Burma Act 1935 (effective 1937) does not happen; Burma is a province in 1947 |
| **P4** | The line must be **geographically sane** | Governing constraint — §3 |

**P1 does the heaviest lifting, and it does it immediately.** Bose's position on partition was
not ambiguous. In *The Indian Struggle* he called Pakistan *"a fantastic plan and an unpractical
proposition,"* on the grounds that *"India is geographically, historically, culturally,
politically and economically an indivisible unit"* and that *"in most parts of India, Hindus and
Muslims are so mixed up that it is not possible to separate them."*

That second clause is the same finding the historical analysis reached from the census data — the
populations were interdigitated at village level and no curve separates them — arrived at from
the other direction, eight years earlier.

**So the question changes shape.** Under these premises there is no internal partition line to
optimise. What there is instead is an **external frontier**, and that is what §4 draws. The
substitution is the answer, not an evasion of it.

---

## 2. Why geographic sanity abolishes the partition line

The previous document catalogued sixteen errors in the Radcliffe Award. Under P4 alone —
without any of the expansionist premises — **most of them do not get solved. They cease to
exist**, because they were all artifacts of dragging a communal line across hydraulic and
economic units that geography had already welded together:

| Historical mistake | Status under a geographically sane rule |
|---|---|
| M10 — canal system severed from headworks | **Dissolved.** The Indus basin is one hydraulic unit; there is nothing to sever. No 1948 water cut-off, no Indus Waters Treaty needed. |
| M14 — East Bengal's jute severed from Calcutta's mills | **Dissolved.** The Ganges–Brahmaputra delta is one unit; crop and mills stay together. |
| M8/M9 — Gurdaspur, Ferozepur/Zira | **Dissolved.** No line runs through Punjab. |
| M15 — 197 enclaves | **Dissolved.** Enclaves are only enclaves relative to a border. |
| M16 — 14–18M displaced, 200k–2M dead | **Dissolved.** Nobody is on the wrong side of anything. |
| M11 — Sikhs statistically invisible | **Dissolved.** Amritsar, Nankana Sahib and Panja Sahib are in one state. |
| M12 — Chittagong Hill Tracts | Survives as an *internal* autonomy question, not a sovereignty one. |

**The internal administrative division should follow basins, not religions:** an Indus province,
a Ganges province, a Bengal–Brahmaputra delta province, a Deccan, an Irrawaddy. Every one of
those is a coherent hydrological and economic organism. Radcliffe's line was insane precisely
because it was drawn perpendicular to every one of them.

---

## 3. The five rules of a geographically sane border

These are the operative constraint from P4. They are not decorative — §4 is derived from them.

**R1 — Crests divide; rivers unite.**
A river in a floodplain is a highway, an irrigation spine, and a shared fishery. It binds its two
banks into one economy. A watershed crest is a genuine barrier. Radcliffe used rivers in plains
(Ravi, Sutlej) as dividers — which is the rule inverted. *Exception:* a river between two deserts,
with no shared command, is a real barrier (this is why the Oxus qualifies and the Sutlej does not).

**R2 — Never sever a hydraulic command from its headworks.**

**R3 — Never split a delta, a doab, or a basin.**

**R4 — No enclaves; no salient narrower than it is deep.**

**R5 — The defender holds the crest and the passes, not the approaches.**
A frontier drawn below the ridgeline means looking uphill at your adversary. This is the Durand
Line's core defect and, in 1962, the Himalayan frontier's.

## 4. The frontier itinerary

Described as a boundary commission would: segment, terrain feature, rationale. West → north →
east → south.

### 4.1 The western frontier — the Oxus

**The Durand Line (1893) is the most geographically insane border on the subcontinent** — worse
than Radcliffe's. It follows no watershed, no river, and no ridge; it bisects the Pashtun homeland;
and in several sectors it sits *below* the crests, violating R5. It has been unenforceable for
over a century.

The British "scientific frontier" debate — Disraeli's term, from 1878 — laid out the real menu:
the Indus, the Sulaiman crest, the Kabul–Ghazni–Kandahar line, or the Oxus. Under P2, the state
advances rather than retreats.

| Segment | Follows | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| W1 | **Amu Darya (Oxus)** from the Pamir knot west to Kelif | R1 exception: a river between two deserts is a true barrier. Frontier with the USSR. |
| W2 | Murghab / Hari Rud to the Iranian plateau edge | Desert margin; no shared command to sever |
| W3 | **Dasht-e Margo and Registan** south to the Makran coast | Sand sea as buffer; frontier with Iran |

**What this buys.** All of Afghanistan (~10–12M in 1947). The Pashtun belt is unified inside one
state, which retires the Durand grievance permanently. Critically, it converts the **Khyber,
Khojak, Bolan and Salang passes from contested gates into interior lines of communication** — the
Hindu Kush ends up *inside* the state rather than athwart its border. On paper this is the most
elegant segment on the map.

**What it costs — and this is the design's fatal segment.** No power has held this ground.
Britain fought three Anglo-Afghan wars and lost an army of roughly 16,000 on the 1842 retreat
from Kabul; the USSR failed 1979–89; the US failed 2001–21. A frontier that is beautiful on a
relief map and unholdable in the field is not a sane frontier — it violates the spirit of R5 even
while satisfying its letter. **If any segment here should be abandoned, it is this one**, falling
back to the Sulaiman–Kirthar crest.

### 4.2 The northern frontier — Kashmir, and the Tibetan plateau

| Segment | Follows | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| N1 | Full J&K; **Karakoram crest**, plus Shaksgam | No Line of Control; no second claimant |
| N2 | **Kunlun Shan** (the Johnson Line of 1865, not the Macartney–MacDonald line of 1899) | Aksai Chin is an endorheic high plateau; the Kunlun is its true northern wall |
| N3 | **Kunlun → Tanggula → Bayan Har**, the northern and eastern rim of the Tibetan plateau | See below |

**Tibet is the decisive move, and it is the one the user's own analogy points at directly.**

In 1947 Tibet was de facto independent and India inherited Britain's treaty position there —
trade agencies at Yatung, Gyantse and Gartok, a military escort at Gyantse, and the Indo-Tibetan
telegraph. Nehru surrendered all of it in the 1954 Panchsheel Agreement. A Bose government does
not.

The geographic argument is decisive and is just R5 at continental scale. **Hold only to the
Himalayan crest and you have a superb wall with an adversary standing on top of it** — the plateau
is the high ground, and every logistical gradient runs downhill toward you. That is exactly the
asymmetry that decided 1962. Take the plateau and the frontier moves to the Kunlun, and *you* hold
the roof.

**The prize is the largest single asset on the board.** Tibet is the Asian Water Tower: the
headwaters of the Indus, Sutlej, Brahmaputra, Salween, Mekong, Yangtze and Yellow rivers. Combined
with an unpartitioned Indus basin, this means **India holds every headwater and every headwork it
depends on.** There is no water dispute available to anyone, in any direction, ever. Set against
the historical timeline — where M10's water crisis took thirteen years and a treaty to patch — this
is the counterfactual's single biggest gain.

**The window is narrow and real.** The PLA entered Tibet in October 1950. To pre-empt it, India
must move in 1947–49, while the Chinese Civil War still has the PLA fully committed. That window
genuinely existed.

### 4.3 The Himalayan states

Nepal, Bhutan and Sikkim lie **entirely on the southern slope** — same watershed, same
physiographic unit, R3 applies. Sikkim was in fact annexed in 1975; Bhutan was already a
protectorate; Nepal had been a subordinate ally since the Treaty of Sugauli (1816).

Under P2 all three are absorbed. **But note the paradox: once Tibet is held, they stop being
frontier states and become interior** — their entire strategic rationale evaporates, which makes
absorbing them the least urgent item on the list rather than the most.

### 4.4 The eastern frontier — Burma

**An honest check on P3 first: Burma is geographically *less* natural to India than the premise
assumes.** The Arakan Yoma and the Chin–Naga–Patkai hills are a genuine barrier — that is *why*
Burma was administratively separated in 1937, and why the WWII land route was so poor that the
Allies had to build the Ledo Road and fly the Hump. Burma is the Irrawaddy basin: a distinct
organism.

**But the compensating gains are large enough to carry it anyway**, and one of them is decisive:

1. **The Siliguri Corridor ceases to exist as a problem.** With an unpartitioned Bengal plus
   Burma, India's northeast is not a 22 km-wide salient hanging off the body of the state — it is
   interior. This is R4, and the Chicken's Neck is **the worst single geographic defect of actual
   post-1947 India**. This premise deletes it.
2. The eastern frontier relocates from the Chin Hills to the **Salween–Mekong divide** — a far
   better natural boundary.
3. The Bay of Bengal becomes an interior sea.

| Segment | Follows | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| E1 | Plateau rim south to the **Salween–Mekong watershed divide** | R1: take the crest, not the river. Frontier with Yunnan and Laos. |
| E2 | Dawna Range / Salween to the Thai frontier | Watershed crest |
| E3 | Tenasserim coast south to the **Isthmus of Kra** | Natural narrowing; the sane terminus |

On the Shan Plateau, appetite says push to the Mekong; **R5 says stop at the divide.** The
watershed is the sane maximum — a border on the Mekong itself would put the frontier in a valley
with the adversary on the ridge above it, which is the Durand mistake repeated in a different
alphabet.

### 4.5 The maritime south

- **Andaman & Nicobar** — already the natural centre of the Bay. Azad Hind had renamed them
  *Shaheed* and *Swaraj* in 1943, so the claim carries its own founding mythology.
- **Ceylon** — never part of British India (a separate crown colony), but 30 km across the Palk
  Strait, joined by Adam's Bridge, and on the Indian continental shelf. Geographically it is part
  of the unit. Under P2 it is absorbed.

**Result: the Bay of Bengal is a closed interior sea**, and India sits astride the western
approach to the Strait of Malacca.

## 5. What the map buys

| Historical problem | Status |
|---|---|
| Partition massacre; 14–18M displaced | **Never happens** |
| Kashmir dispute; 1947, 1965, 1999 | **No second claimant** |
| Indus water dispute; IWT (1960) | **No dispute possible** — one basin, one state |
| Brahmaputra / Farakka anxieties | **Dissolved** — headwaters held |
| Siliguri Corridor vulnerability | **Dissolved** |
| Durand Line insurgency | **Dissolved** — Pashtuns unified |
| 1962 Himalayan defeat | **Inverted** — India holds the plateau |
| Bay of Bengal as contested space | **Interior sea** |

Land frontiers with the USSR (Oxus), Iran, China (Kunlun), Thailand and Indochina. India becomes
a principal in Central Asian politics rather than an object of them.

## 6. What the map costs

A frontier plan that does not name its own liabilities is propaganda, not analysis. Four:

### 6.1 The method the premise invokes

P2 names the PRC in Tibet and Xinjiang as the model. That model has specific content: the 1950
invasion of Tibet, the suppression of the 1959 uprising, the destruction of monastic institutions
and the Dalai Lama's flight; and in Xinjiang from 2017, documented mass internment, forced
assimilation, and suppression of Uyghur language and religious practice.

Applied here, the same methods land on Pashtuns, Tibetans, Baloch, Shan, Karen, Kachin, Sinhalese
and Nepalis. **The map in §4 is drawn on the assumption that this is done.** That is what "filling
its stomach" consists of when it is written out as a line item rather than a metaphor — and it is
worth being clear that the humanitarian ledger is not simply positive just because the partition
massacre is deleted from it. One catastrophe is traded for a longer, quieter one.

### 6.2 Afghanistan

The Oxus frontier is the prettiest segment on the map and the least defensible in practice
(§4.1). Britain, the USSR and the US each failed there in turn. **This is the segment to cut.**

### 6.3 Permanent structural conflict with China

Holding Tibet does not avoid the Sino-Indian conflict — it makes it existential and permanent
rather than episodic. 1962 does not happen; something larger and unending replaces it.

### 6.4 The premise's own fragility

Burma and Ceylon both had mature independence movements — Aung San and the AFPFL, the Ceylon
National Congress — so P3 buys a colonial war, not a province. And Bose died in a plane crash at
Taipei in August 1945. To the Allies he was an Axis collaborator; the INA trials at the Red Fort
in 1945–46 were prosecutions for treason. **For him to take power in 1947 requires either an Axis
victory or a British capitulation to a man they had just tried as a traitor.** This is the
scenario's true load-bearing assumption, and it is the weakest one in it.

---

## 7. The finding

Ranked by how much each premise actually earns:

1. **P4 (geographic sanity) is worth more than P2 (appetite).** Simply refusing to draw a
   communal line across the Indus and Ganges–Brahmaputra basins deletes fourteen of the sixteen
   historical errors and the entire death toll — *before any territory is annexed at all*. The
   cheapest enormous gain on the board requires no conquest whatsoever.
2. **Tibet is the one expansionist move that is geographically correct, strategically decisive,
   and was genuinely available** in the 1947–50 window.
3. **Burma's real value is not Burma. It is the deletion of the Siliguri Corridor** — a defect
   that has constrained Indian strategy for eight decades.
4. **Afghanistan is where the map is most beautiful and the plan is worst.** Cut it.

**The geographically sane maximum: no internal partition; the Sulaiman–Kirthar crest in the west;
the Kunlun and the Tibetan plateau rim in the north; the Salween–Mekong divide in the east; the
Bay of Bengal as an interior sea.**

Not the Oxus. Appetite has to stop somewhere, and the sane place to stop it is at the last crest
you can actually stand on.
